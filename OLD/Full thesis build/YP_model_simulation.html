Yersinia pestis Cellular Pathogenesis

Modeling intracellular and extracellular dynamics during macrophage interaction

Summary

Yersinia pestis passes through two phases inside a mammalian host. What a flea bite delivers is a vector-adapted population, unarmed for the host it has just entered: the bacteria are readily taken up by macrophages, and within the spacious Yersinia-containing vacuole they replicate under body temperature, reduced pH and oxidative stress. Those stresses are also signals. They induce the F1 capsule, the pH 6 antigen and the Ysc–Yop type III secretion system, and the cell that needed invasive factors to get in now presents a surface that refuses re-entry. How the intracellular phase ends — by which host-cell death programme, and on what timing — remains unresolved.

Interpretation

Each macrophage here runs a two-type birth–death process with one-way conversion: early bacteria (blue) replicate and mature into the adapted type (red), and any resident bacterium can trigger rupture, so containment fails at a rate proportional to the load the cell is carrying. A burst frees the whole cohort at once. Loose early-type bacteria may be taken up again; adapted ones refuse, and arriving neutrophils clear only the former. Rupture is phenotype-blind here — one rate for both types.
