  RELEASE OF VIRUS AND BACTERIA FROM INFECTED CELLS:
           MODELS OF BUDDING AND BURSTING
     ADAM ALDRIDGE, BEVELYNN WHALER, MARTÍN LÓPEZ GARCÍA, CARMEN
              MOLINA PARÍS, JOE GILLARD AND GRANT LYTHE∗

     Abstract. When are virus or bacteria released from an infected cell? We consider two types
of mathematical model. In the first, “budding”, infected cells are assumed to release new infectious
particles at a constant rate (that is, constant probability per unit time). No description of the
intracellular dynamics is needed; the mean number of new infectious particles released per infected
cell is simply the rate of release multiplied by the mean lifetime of an infected cell. In the second,
“bursting”, infectious particles accumulate inside a host cell until the cell dies and the intracellular
load is released, all at once. At the level of an individual cell, the simplest stochastic budding models
have two types of independent event: release of an infectious particle and death of the infected cell.
In bursting, however, release of infectious particles and death of the host cell are not independent
events: they occur simultaneously. We construct bursting models in which the death of an infected
cell, releasing its cargo, is an event whose rate, like increase or decrease of the intracellular population,
is proportional to the population size. Mean quantities describing release of new infectious particles
in the bursting model satisfy simple differential equations that yield exact expressions for burst
size distributions, and mean burst size conditioned on time of release. Even though the model has
no imposed carrying capacity, but the mean intracellular population, given that the burst has not
happened before time t, is an increasing function of time with a finite limiting value.


     1. Introduction. Viruses, some bacteria, and bacteriophages use the environ-
ment inside host cells to reproduce, then exit and spread to other host cells. In the
case of enveloped viruses [1], once a virus is assembled inside a host cell, its membrane
can fuse with, and then pinch off from, the cell membrane. Thus, fully-formed virus
particles can be released, one at a time, without cell lysis. On the other hand, exit
of non-enveloped viruses is observed to occur in bursts, associated with lysis of the
cell provoked by the presence of the virus [2]. This process has been observed in the
case of bacteriophages since the early days of laboratory virology [3]. Some bacteria
also reproduce inside cells. The bacterium Francisella tularensis is phagocytosed by
alveolar macrophages, but is able to survive and escape from the phagosome to the
cytosol [4, 5]. After multiple rounds of division in the cytosol, the bacterial load
eventually causes the host macrophage to rupture and die, releasing many infectious
bacteria [6, 7]. B. anthracis spores germinate in alveolar macrophages, replicate in
the cytosol and are released when a cell ruptures and dies. These two schemes for
the release of virus or bacteria, budding and bursting, require two different types of
mathematical models [8]. Newly replicated and released particles may stand a better
chance of survival if they move to other host cells en masse, than if they are released
one at a time [9].
     The number of new infectious particles released per infected cell is an important
quantity in determining whether a small number of pathogenic agents inside a human
or animal host will grow into a detectable infection. The number is an integer-valued
random variable whose full distribution can only be calculated from the stochastic
intracellular dynamics, even if the mean value may be determined from ordinary
differential equations (ODEs). Similarly, reproduction numbers are integer-valued
random variables whose distributions are determined by the stochastic dynamics of
release and absorption [10].
     A simplification used in most viral infection models is that the rate of release of
new infectious particles is proportional to the number of infected cells [11–13]. That

is, all infected cells are treated equally, regardless of how long they have been infected
or how many infectious particles they hold. If V represents extracellular infectious
particles and I represents infected cells, then the rate of change of V includes a
positive term pI, where p is a constant. If the mean lifetime of an infected cell is 1/δ,
then the mean number of new infectious particles released per infected cell is given
by p/δ. Because of the linearity of these relationships, they can be taken to apply
when V and I are interpreted as concentrations per unit volume, as the average over
a cohort of cells, or as applying to a very small number of infected cells at the early
stage of an infection. At the level of an individual cell, however, the dynamics is
stochastic and the assumption behind such a “continuous production” model is that
two types of event (release of an infectious particle and death of the infected cell) are
independent. The events are not independent in models of bursting, where infectious
particles accumulate inside a host cell until the cell dies and the whole intracellular
load is released at once [8, 14].
     At the stochastic level of an individual cell, the simplest budding model is that,
in a time interval of duration ∆t, an infected cell may release an infectious particle,
with probability p∆t, or die, with probability δ∆t. The budding and death rates, p
and δ, do not depend on the number of infectious particles inside the cell, as long
as there is at least one. The assumption can be modified by assuming that not all
cells are productively infected, that infected cells pass through an “eclipse phase”
before they begin to release infectious particles [15], or that infectious particles are
released in cohorts [16–18]. We must modify the assumption in a more fundamental
way in order to model bursting: the mechanism by which multiple infectious particles
are released, all at once, when the infected cell ruptures and dies [19]. Release of
infectious particles and death of infected cells are not independent processes. They
are, rather, simultaneous.
     Including intracellular dynamics, for example letting the rate of release of viral
particles, or the cellular death rate, depend on the time a cell has been infected [20–23],
tends to make a model more complicated. In one case, however, where the viral RNA
inside each cell is governed by a linear ODE, it is possible to replace an age-structured
model by an ODE [24, 25]. To construct a mathematical model of bursting, it may
be assumed that the number of infectious particles released when a cell dies is a
fixed parameter N [26], but the time at which the cell dies is a random variable.
Alternatively, if the accumulation of infectious particles in an infected cell over time
is explicitly modelled, and the time of cell death is an independent random variable,
then the number of infectious particles released is also a random variable [10, 27].
     In this work, we use an explicit stochastic model of the accumulation of infectious
particles in an infected cell over time. Our bursting model of the intracellular popula-
tion of virus or bacteria is a birth-and-death process with an extra type of event called
“catastrophe” or “killing” where the population is eliminated [28–30]. The greater
the load of infectious particles, the greater is the risk of the cell-death “catastrophe”.
Each intracellular infectious particle has division rate λ. The model also contains a
third type of event: death or removal of an intracellular infectious particle with rate
µ, which makes it possible that a cell may cease to be infected altogether.
     In the birth-death-catastrophe model of the intracellular population, the number
of intracellular particles at time t is the integer-valued random variable Xt . The
random variables characterising the burst are

     3. Bursting. To model bursting, we construct the following explicit model of the
intracellular population. The number of intracellular particles at time t is the discrete-
valued random variable Xt that can take values 0, 1, 2, . . . and H. The notation
Xt = H means that the cell has ruptured and released its intracellular content.
Within the Markove process, the absording states 0 and H could be merged into one
state, but we maintain the distinction for convenience when extending to models with
natural cells death (from state 0) or reinfection of released particles.
     If there are Xt intracellular particles at time t, and (λ + µ + α)∆t  1, then one
of three types of event may occur before t + ∆t.
       1. There may be one more particle at time t + ∆t, with probability λXt ∆t.
       2. There may be one fewer at time t + ∆t, with probability µXt ∆t.
       3. The host cell may die before Xt+∆t , releasing Xt infectious particles, with
          probability αXt ∆t.

     4. Discussion. The number of new infectious particles released per infected
cell [10] is an integer-valued random variable that is key to determining whether a
small number of pathogenic agents inside a human or animal host will grow into a
detectable infection. It may be possible to calculate the mean value using ODEs
with simple assumptions, but calculating the full distribution requires considering the
intracellular dynamics. Here, we compare two models: budding and bursting. Both
types of models use the variables, V (t) and I(t), that represent free virus and infected
cells. To model bursting, we use an explicit stochastic model of the accumulation
of infectious particles in an infected cell over time. The model is a birth-and-death
process with an extra type of event called “catastrophe” [28–30]. Despite the explicit
stochastic representation of intracellular dynamics, and increased number of variables
compared to the budding model, we find simple analytical expressions for burst-size
distributions, means of time-dependent quantities and conditional probabilities.
     Data obtained when mice were challenged with Francisella tularensis SCHU S4,
yielded estimated λ, µ and α values of 0.15 h−1 , 0.01 h−1 , and 0.00015 h−1 [30]. The
predicted fraction of cells that do not release any bacteria, equal to the quantity a
in (3.24), is less than 7 percent, and the distribution of burst sizes from cells that
rupture was predicted to be geometric with typical burst sizes of hundreds of bac-
teria [7, 35]. A consequence is that a dose as low as 10 CFU of Francisella tularensis
can result in infection [36]. Recently, Rawsthorne-Manning et al. (private communica-
tion; manuscript in preparation), observed J774 cells heavily infected with Francisella
tularensis. Video recording of bursting events with en masse bacterial release, and
use of high-magnification confocal microscopy to count bacterial payload within cells
that were adjudged to be close to rupturing, provide observational support to the
burst-size prediction.
     Very different parameter estimates are found for Bacillus anthracis. Approximate
Bayesian Computation Sequential Monte Carlo using published experimental time-
series data produced the estimates λ ≈ 0.64 h−1 , µ ≈ 1.64 h−1 , and α ≈ 0.04 h−1 [37].
Consequently, more than 96percent of phagocytes are predicted to eliminate an in-
tracellular Bacillus anthracis infection. Using (3.25), the remaining fraction of pha-
gocytes that do rupture are predicted to release only an average of 1.6 bacteria each,
consistent with estimates that the dose required for infection is between 8 × 103 and
5 × 104 spores. The dynamics of coxiella burnetti, which adopts a small form outside
a host cell and a large form inside, may be similar. Stochastic population dynamics
has been used to model the highly variable timing and magnitude of HIV release
from reactivated memory CD4 T cells from infected donors [14]: on some days more
than 10000 HIV RNA copies were found in single-cell well supernatant, on other days
nothing was detected.

     The budding model is consistent with the assumption is that every infected cell be-
haves independently, selecting one of two possible events (virion release or cell death)
in each time interval. In bursting, on the other hand, infectious particles accumulate
inside a host cell until the cell dies and the intracellular load is released at once. Then,
release of infectious particles and death of the host cell are not independent events,
but occur simultaneously. Our two scenarios of budding and bursting can be thought
of as opposite ends of a spectrum of possibilities. Intermediate scenarios to consider
include internal compartments that provide mechanisms for release of multiple infec-
tious particles without immediate lysis of the host cell [38]. Such vesicles may enhance
infectivity by allowing multiple viral genomes to enter a nearby cell [39]. A related
challenge is producing models of viruses, including plant viruses, that are transmitted
